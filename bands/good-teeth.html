✳ The Band

GOOD TEETH consists of five distinct musicians with different tastes, personalities, and musical instincts. They were not assembled to fill predetermined roles in a marketable concept. Each member already had an audience; the label supplied the resources and gave them room to work.

Nobody dictated a genre, image, sound, or songwriting formula. There was no prescribed identity they had to grow into, no sound they were expected to reproduce, and no instruction to make their existing audiences fit together neatly.

The assignment was simple: five musicians, a pile of resources, and the freedom to make something together.

They did. The result became GOOD TEETH.

✳ The Sound of Creator

GOOD TEETH's music feels like Creator itself made into widely accessible music: strange, beautiful, filthy, emotional, technological, organic, catchy, aggressive, playful, melancholic, and impossible to confine to one genre.

Its appeal crosses species, cultures, ages, and musical tastes. A song can be immediate and infectious without being simple, technically complicated without becoming inaccessible, or brutally heavy without losing its melody.

These influences are not separate phases or neatly divided styles. They collide inside individual songs. Electronics can turn abrasive, party music can become sincere, beautiful melodies can break into screaming, and heavy passages can open into something delicate.

Immediate

Hooks, memorable melodies, infectious rhythms, and music that gets stuck in your head.

Unpredictable

Distortion, technical passages, strange textures, abrupt changes in intensity, and unexpected turns.

Emotional

Melancholy underneath bright production, vulnerability inside aggression, and sincerity where nobody expected it.

✳ Five Musicians, One Band

Each member brings a distinct musical instinct. Their contributions matter individually, but GOOD TEETH's sound emerges from the way those contributions interact.

Ives

26 · Vocals

Ives has an absurd vocal range and an equally broad range of delivery. He can sound like a polished popular frontman, a cocky trashy party singer, or a genuinely beautiful and vulnerable vocalist. He can shout, rasp, and unleash full hardcore screams without losing the thread of the song.

His voice can move between those modes inside a single track. A sudden change in delivery can transform a section's emotional meaning, turning swagger into desperation or a beautiful melody into something ferocious.

Renga

24 · Synth / Keyboard

Renga builds the band's electronic architecture: strange melodies, digital textures, loops, effects, atmospheric layers, bright hooks, distorted electronics, and unusual rhythmic ideas.

His parts can be beautiful, playful, futuristic, melancholy, abrasive, or completely bizarre. He gives GOOD TEETH much of its unmistakably strange character, using electronics as a source of melody, atmosphere, rhythm, and aggression.

Tōgo

22 · Electric Guitar

Tōgo moves between celestial atmospheric playing and fucking sick technical riffs. He uses loops, effects, layered harmonics, melodic passages, fast playing, and sudden guitar explosions.

His guitar can function like another synthesizer, weaving itself into Renga's electronic arrangements, or become the band's sharpest weapon. He can make a section feel enormous and weightless before tearing straight through it.

Purl

21 · Drums

Purl is the physical engine of the band. He can drive catchy music, support vulnerable passages, follow complicated guitar parts, lock tightly with Riley, reinforce electronic rhythms, and build tension without overwhelming the arrangement.

Then he can absolutely obliterate the kit during a heavy section. Hardcore intensity is always available to him, whether the song begins as a polished electronic hook or a delicate melodic passage.

Riley

20 · Bass

Riley provides emotional and harmonic gravity. His bass rarely just follows the guitar. He creates melancholy underneath bright music, reinforces heavy sections, and interacts closely with Purl to shape the physical movement of a song.

His playing can be restrained without being unimportant. A bass line can change the emotional meaning of an otherwise ridiculous track, making it unexpectedly sad, or give a heavy passage its weight without demanding attention for itself.

✳ Nobody Owns a Genre

GOOD TEETH does not divide its sound into individual genre assignments. No member is responsible for a single mood, style, or level of intensity. Hardcore belongs to everyone.

Electronics Can Bite

Renga can make digital textures distorted, abrasive, rhythmic, and aggressive rather than merely atmospheric or decorative.

The Low End Has Feelings

Riley can make a bright or absurd song unexpectedly melancholy, or give a heavy arrangement its emotional and physical weight.

Guitar Can Become Anything

Tōgo can build shimmering atmosphere, intricate melodic passages, technical riffs, and sudden bursts of overwhelming noise.

The Kit Is Not Safe

Purl can hold a delicate rhythm together or turn a song into a full-force physical assault without losing his connection to the arrangement.

The Voice Can Change the Whole Song

Ives can move from polished pop vocals to cocky party delivery, vulnerability, rasp, shouting, and full hardcore screaming as the music demands. Those shifts belong to one singer and one continuous performance.

✳ Contrast Is the Point

GOOD TEETH relies on contrast, not as a novelty trick but as a central part of its musical identity. Their songs can change emotional and sonic direction dramatically while still feeling like coherent pieces of music.

The Hook

A polished, catchy opening with bright electronics, accessible melodies, and enough momentum to get stuck in someone's head.

The Shift

Distortion builds. Guitar parts become technical, the rhythm grows more aggressive, and electronic textures begin to bite.

The Collision

Purl drives the kit harder, Riley makes the low end hit, Tōgo tears into the arrangement, Renga transforms the electronics, and Ives may break into a full scream.

The Collapse

The intensity can suddenly give way to quiet vulnerability. A trashy party song might turn sincere; a beautiful section might erupt into screaming; a heavy passage might resolve into an unexpectedly melodic moment.

None of these shifts has to follow a fixed sequence. The contrast works because the musicians are responding to each other, not following a formula for when the heavy part is supposed to happen.

✳ How They Make Music

Their arrangements are collaborative and reactive. Ideas change as they pass between the five musicians. An electronic hook can inspire a guitar part; the bass can change its emotional meaning; the drums can reshape its rhythm; a vocal decision can transform the entire section.

Build, Break, Rebuild

They rehearse, rewrite, experiment with sounds, argue over arrangements, improvise, extend sections, and try ideas that may not work. A song is something they actively discover together rather than a fixed blueprint handed down by one person.

Keep the Accident

Not every useful idea begins as a plan. Sometimes an unexpected sound, mistake, or improvised change works better than the original arrangement. Those happy accidents can become part of the music.

Listen to Each Other

Their individual parts are not isolated performances stacked together. Each musician can respond to a change in another part, shifting their own contribution and changing where the song goes next.

Make It Work Live

The arrangements leave room for interaction and movement. A section can grow, change intensity, or take a different route during a performance because the musicians are playing with one another in real time.

✳ Live Performances

GOOD TEETH's performances feel live rather than perfectly reproduced. The band moves, sweats, reacts, communicates through glances, makes mistakes, improvises, extends sections, alters vocal phrasing, builds loops, and changes intensity in response to the crowd and each other.

Their songs are recognizable, but the performance is not simply a flawless recreation of the recording. A familiar section might stretch longer than expected. A transition might become heavier. A vocal phrase might land differently. The musicians can take a moment somewhere unexpected and bring the song back together.

The recordings contain the music. The performances contain five musicians making that music happen right now.

✳ Fame on Creator

GOOD TEETH is the most popular band on Creator. Their music revolutionized the planet's music scene, reaching audiences across species, cultures, ages, and musical tastes.

Their fame is enormous, but celebrity culture on Creator does not operate like an Earth-style spectacle. Being famous does not mean a person stops being part of ordinary life.

Recognized, Not Sealed Away

People recognize them, listen to them, discuss them, take photographs, and occasionally approach them. Recognition is part of their lives, but it does not automatically turn every public appearance into an event.

Still People in Public

They can eat at restaurants, walk through neighborhoods, shop, travel, and spend time in public without constant paparazzi, limo convoys, bodyguards, or a permanent celebrity bubble.

Famous Across Creator

Their audience crosses regional and cultural boundaries. Their popularity is not confined to one scene, one species, or one particular kind of listener.

Ordinary Life Continues

Their careers are extraordinary. Their daily lives can still contain restaurants, errands, travel, conversations, work, and all the unremarkable activities that make up a life.

✳ What Makes GOOD TEETH

GOOD TEETH is not defined by electronic music, hardcore, technical guitar, emo, pop hooks, or any one of the sounds that appear in its songs. Its defining quality is the collision between five different musicians who were given the freedom to make something together.

Renga can make the music strange. Riley can make it ache. Tōgo can make it soar or hit like a weapon. Purl can give it a body. Ives can make it beautiful, ridiculous, vicious, or vulnerable. Any of them can take the song somewhere the others did not expect.

The result is music that can feel alien and immediately familiar, technically intricate and effortlessly catchy, filthy and sincere, playful and devastating. It can be all of those things without having to choose which one it is.

Five different musicians were given freedom to make something together. The collision became the sound of Creator.
